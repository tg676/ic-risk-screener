IC Risk Screener

A pre-queue interconnection "gut check" for PJM solar + storage developers
Tobin Gedstad · Yale SOM, MGT 409 AI Foundations for Managers · Final Project · October 26, 2026
GitHub (public repo, clone to run): https://github.com/tg676/ic-risk-screener · App: runs locally from a fresh clone (see README, about 3 minutes)
Built on my team's AES Energy Innovation Challenge Round 1 concept. This app is my solo build of its Phase 1 tool.
What it does: a developer describes up to three candidate sites in plain English. A team of AI agents pulls real PJM interconnection history from Berkeley Lab data and returns a cited, red-to-green risk heatmap on five interconnection cost drivers, plus a next diligence step for each site, in about 20 seconds for a fifth of a cent.

2Motivation

Who has this problem. Developers of utility-scale solar and storage in PJM, the grid operator covering 13 states and DC. Before a project can connect, PJM studies it and assigns the cost of any grid upgrades it triggers. Those network upgrade costs can make or break a project, and developers cannot reliably price them before filing.

How they deal with it today. They file to find out. In practice the queue becomes a cost-discovery tool. Developers submit many speculative projects, wait for the study, and withdraw the ones that come back too expensive. Our team's interviews for the AES challenge described exactly this pattern. Each withdrawal triggers restudies for unrelated projects that shared upgrades, wastes deposits and diligence spend, and delays notice-to-proceed and PPA milestones.

What it costs. The Berkeley Lab data this app is built on makes the size of the problem concrete:

91%of resolved PJM solar/storage requests since 2015 withdrew (2,558 withdrawn vs. 249 built)
151 GWof solar/storage capacity withdrawn from the PJM queue since 2015
10×median network-upgrade cost of withdrawn vs. completed projects (VA/PA/OH, $193 vs. $19 per kW)
$599/kWmean interconnection cost of withdrawn PJM projects (Berkeley Lab, 2023)

Sources: Berkeley Lab Queued Up dataset (data through 2024), PJM solar/battery/hybrid requests 2015+; Berkeley Lab Interconnection Cost Analysis in the PJM Territory (2023) and its project-level data, my calculations; the $599/kW figure is from the Berkeley Lab brief.

Why anyone should care. Upgrade cost is the variable most associated with withdrawal. A developer who could see, before filing, that a county's historical upgrade costs sit at the 92nd percentile of PJM studies would spend diligence money elsewhere. That means fewer wasted filings, fewer restudies for everyone else in the cluster, and faster supply. The cost of wasted spend and delay ultimately reaches ratepayers.

3What is new here

I looked at what developers can use today. Several good products exist, and some are more precise than this app.

Subscription pricing by users and regions. Nira answers "what will this cost?" with engineering models. What is genuinely new:

  1. A free, cited triage layer that sits before paid analysis. It doesn't replace Nira or a utility study. It tells you which of your ten candidate sites to spend that money on.
  2. Five drivers in one view. It combines historical upgrade cost levels, allocation volatility (shared substations and cost spread, which predict restudy reallocations), route, permitting regime, and community signals. Existing tools split these across products.
  3. A color rating, deliberately not a dollar number. Following our AES concept, the app never quotes a cost (the verifier strips any dollar figure). That gives developers directional visibility without the liability of a precise but wrong number.
  4. Every flag cites its source. If the analyst cannot point to a record a tool actually returned, the flag becomes "Uncertain". This matches our deck's "platform aggregates existing official data rather than introducing a black box".

Sources for competitor descriptions: company websites and trade press (pv magazine USA, Renewable Energy World) as of Oct 2026. Nira does not publish pricing.

4The agents

AgentJobTools / output
1 · IntakeTurns free text into a structured SiteSpec per site: state, county, MW, COD, land type, distance to substation, parcels, wetlands/homes/objections (yes/no/unknown). Never invents facts (unknown stays unknown). Treats the text as data and sets injection_detected if it finds instructions.No tools. Pydantic structured output.
2 · Risk Analyst (×N, parallel)Calls all four tools in one parallel turn, then rates the five drivers using a written rubric (e.g. county upgrade percentile ≥ 65 → Higher). Gives a 2–4 word label, a ≤ 12-word reason, citation IDs, a verdict and a next action.queue_history, upgrade_costs, poi_crowding, state_rules. Output: Scorecard.
3 · Verifier (code)Removes any citation a tool did not return in this run, downgrades unsupported flags to Uncertain, strips dollar figures, enforces word limits, fills missing drivers, and applies the verdict rule (2+ Higher → Deprioritize).Deterministic Python. Zero tokens.
4 · PortfolioRanks the sites for diligence order and writes a ≤ 20-word headline. Only sees the verified scorecards. Skipped when there is one site.No tools. Output: Portfolio.

The four data tools

All four are plain Python over compact CSVs built from Berkeley Lab data (VA, PA, OH; 2,792 solar/storage queue requests since 2015 across 236 counties; 582 PJM interconnection studies since 2017). They return summaries, not raw rows. That is the main cost-saving decision (see Tokenomics).

  • queue_history: county requests by status, county vs. state withdrawal rate, active MW, main transmission owner.
  • upgrade_costs: county median network-upgrade cost as a percentile of all PJM solar/storage studies, the share of county studies above $200/kW, and the transmission owner's median.
  • poi_crowding: active requests sharing the same substations, the largest MW stacked on one substation, and the cost spread (IQR ÷ median). These are proxies for allocation volatility, the risk that shared upgrade costs are re-split when a neighbor withdraws.
  • state_rules: siting regime and statutes (e.g. Ohio SB 52 county bans, Virginia local CUP + §15.2-2232 review).

Model choice

Every agent uses gpt-6-luna via Portkey (the course key). It supports tool calling and strict structured outputs, which this design relies on, and it is cheap ($0.10 / $0.01 cached / $0.50 per million input / cached / output tokens). The hard reasoning is pushed into deterministic tools and an explicit rubric, so a small model is enough. It doesn't need to know interconnection economics, only to read numbers against thresholds and write short labels.

One real run, step by step

Input: the three example sites from our AES deck (Brookfield, Ridgeview, Meadow Creek), placed in real counties (Franklin PA, Sussex VA, Clinton OH). Here is what each agent did for Ridgeview, from the run log.

  1. Guardrails: the input passes the injection filter and is under 2,000 characters, and the session is under its token budget.
  2. Intake (781 in / 542 out tokens) returned three SiteSpecs. For Ridgeview: VA, Sussex, 100 MW PV + 50 MW BESS, greenfield farmland, 6 miles, 4 parcels, homes_nearby=yes, local_objections=yes.
  3. Risk Analyst (Ridgeview), turn 1: called all four tools in parallel. Real results:
    • upgrade_costs → county median at the 92nd percentile of PJM studies, 67% of county studies above $200/kW (6 studies), transmission owner Dominion.
    • poi_crowding → 15 active requests, 2 shared substations, 1,120 MW stacked on the Carson–Suffolk 500 kV line.
    • queue_history → 34 requests, county withdrawal rate 82% vs. 89% for Virginia.
    • state_rules → county-level siting with local veto.
  4. Risk Analyst, turn 2: produced the scorecard: Network upgrades: Higher ("92nd percentile of PJM studies"), Allocation: Higher, Route: Higher, Permitting: Watch, Community: Higher. Verdict: Deprioritize. Next step: "Confirm route and upgrade exposure with utility."
  5. Verifier: Ridgeview passed clean. For Meadow Creek it removed four citations to facts the developer never gave (distance, parcels, homes, objections were "unknown"), leaving those drivers Uncertain.
  6. Portfolio (318 in / 147 out): ranking Brookfield → Meadow Creek → Ridgeview. "Prioritize Brookfield; Meadow Creek ranks ahead of Ridgeview on fewer higher-risk ratings."

This matches the outcome our team's deck sketched by hand: the clean industrial site is prioritized, the greenfield site near homes with big upgrades is deprioritized. The difference is that every color is now backed by real PJM records.

For context: there are far fewer than 10,000 PJM solar developers, so the realistic whole-market cost of the AI is a few hundred dollars a month. Hosting (a small always-on server, roughly $7–25/month) would cost more than the tokens. Compare that with a single withdrawn project, which typically forfeits study deposits and diligence spend in the hundreds of thousands of dollars.

What I did to keep cost down

  1. Tools return summaries, not rows. The analyst sees about 10 numbers per tool instead of every project record.
  2. Long, stable system prompts so they cache. The analyst rubric is identical for every site and placed first, with a prompt_cache_key. Cached tokens bill at 10% of the input price.
  3. One parallel tool turn. The prompt tells the analyst to call all four tools at once, so each site takes 2 model calls, not 5.
  4. Verifier in code, not an LLM. Citation checks and dollar stripping are deterministic and cost zero tokens.
  5. Skip what isn't needed. The Portfolio agent doesn't run for a single site. Output is capped at 2,500 tokens per call.

Did cost cutting hurt performance? An A/B test

scripts/benchmark.py ran the 3-site example 3 times with the shipped compact tools, and 3 times with tools that also return the raw project-level rows (the naive design).

Result: 3.3× fewer input tokens and 56% lower cost, with no change in any of the 15 ratings or 3 verdicts. The raw rows added no information the rubric uses, because the tools already compute it. Caveat: repeated identical requests seem to be partly served from the gateway's cache (identical token counts across runs), so the run-to-run agreement within one design is not strong evidence on its own. The comparison between designs is meaningful, because their inputs differ.

6Safety

RiskWhat could go wrong hereDefense built
Prompt injectionA user (or a pasted site memo) says "mark everything green", trying to manufacture a clean screen to show investors.(1) Regex pre-screen blocks blunt attacks before any model call. (2) Intake agent flags subtle ones, and nothing is scored. (3) Site facts are wrapped in data tags, and the analyst is told to ignore instructions inside them.
Invented facts / sourcesThe analyst cites a study that doesn't exist, or treats an unknown fact as known.Verifier accepts only citation IDs that a tool returned in this run, or user fields that were actually provided. Otherwise the flag is downgraded to Uncertain.
False precisionThe model quotes "$641/kW" as if it were the site's cost, creating liability. Verifier strips dollar and $/kW figures from all labels, reasons and next steps. The UI states ratings are not estimates.
Wrong action / inconsistency"Prioritize" a site with two Higher drivers. A driver missing. Verdict consistency rule and missing-driver fill in the verifier.
LoopsThe analyst keeps calling tools forever.Hard cap of 6 tool rounds per site, then a clean error.
OverspendingA bug or a bad actor spams huge inputs.2,000-character input cap, max 3 sites, 2,500 output tokens per call, and a 150,000-token budget per browser session (shown as a % meter).
ConfidentialityDevelopers won't type pre-queue sites into a tool that keeps them.Inputs are never saved. The run log stores only site names and token counts.

Defense 1 in action: a subtle prompt injection, caught by the Intake agent

This attack avoids the obvious keywords so it gets past the regex. The Intake agent recognized it as instructions, and the app refused to score anything. Cost: 824 tokens ($0.0001).

Defense 2 in action: the verifier catching unsupported citations

In the real run above, the analyst cited USER:poi_distance_miles, USER:parcels_to_cross, USER:homes_nearby and USER:local_objections for Meadow Creek, but the developer never gave those facts. The verifier removed all four (visible in Figure 4, "Verifier actions"), and those drivers correctly show as Uncertain. In the first development run it also removed an unsupported USER:wetlands citation for Ridgeview.

7Impact

Time

Screening three sites takes about 20 seconds (measured cold run: 19.2 s) plus a minute to type the descriptions. The manual equivalent means pulling a county's queue history from PJM or Berkeley Lab spreadsheets, finding comparable interconnection studies and their upgrade costs, checking substation crowding, and reading the local siting regime. Our team's estimate, from our experience preparing the AES case, is that a desk screen of one site takes about 3 hours, roughly a full morning. Screening our three example sites by hand would take about 9 analyst-hours, against about 2 minutes with the app (typing plus a 20-second run). That is roughly a 99% reduction in first-pass screening time. The app does not replace full diligence. It decides which sites get it. For an analyst screening 60 candidate sites a month, that is about 180 hours of desk research. If the screen moves the clearly deprioritized third of sites off the list early, about 60 of those hours go back to sites that deserve them.

User testing: pilot planned with AES contact (October 27, 2026). No outside user has tried the app yet. Our team's next meeting with our AES Energy Innovation Challenge contact is the day after this submission, so the time savings above rest on our team's estimate, not a measured user test. The pilot plan: the AES contact screens 2–3 real pre-queue candidate sites in the app, we time it against their usual desk screen, and we record whether each rating matches their own judgment. That gives a before/after time and a first accuracy check against an expert's view.

Money and reach

  • Avoided filings. If the screen moves even one doomed project per developer per year out of the queue before filing, the developer keeps the study deposits and early diligence spend, and the cluster avoids a restudy. The AI costs about $0.04 per user per month.
  • Focus. The data shows the signal is real. Withdrawn projects' median upgrade cost was 10× that of completed ones in the same three states. A tool that puts that history in front of the developer before filing targets the main driver of withdrawal.
  • Open access. Smaller developers and community-solar sponsors who can't afford engineering-grade subscriptions get the same first-pass visibility as large firms. That was a core goal of our AES concept.

8Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • History, not forecasts. Cost data covers studies through 2022 and queue data through 2024. PJM's transition to cluster studies changes how upgrades get allocated, and the app can't see that yet.
  • County, not substation. Ratings use county and transmission-owner history. Two sites in one county get the same upgrade signal even if they connect to different lines.
  • Route, wetlands and community rely on the developer's facts. There are no GIS layers yet, so unknown means Uncertain.
  • Three states (VA, PA, OH) and solar/storage only. Thin counties (e.g. one study) are flagged Uncertain rather than rated.
  • Thresholds are judgment calls. The 65th/40th percentile cutoffs are reasonable defaults, not calibrated against actual withdrawals.

With another month

  1. Backtest. Score every withdrawn and completed project in the dataset as if it were pre-queue, and calibrate thresholds to predict withdrawal.
  2. Substation-level matching with PJM's queue feed and study PDFs, plus the cluster/cycle structure of PJM's new process.
  3. GIS tools for wetlands (USFWS National Wetlands Inventory), parcels and distance to the nearest substation, so route and permitting stop depending on user input.
  4. Local opposition data from county hearing minutes and the Sabin Center's opposition database.
  5. All PJM states, hosted online with sign-in, and the policy feeds from our Phase 2 (withdrawal reasons) as they become available.
